6.1 Summary

The work preserited in this thesis is not al1 original. Part of it was done before and al1
we did in this case was to verify the existing results. CVe gave a derivation for the various
quantities of interest in the detection process such as the sigriül-to-noise ratio. the anibi-
guity function. and the fitting factor (see chapter 2 and the relevant sections within). The
quadrupole formula and the radiation react ion for gravitat ionally bound systems were de-
rived in chapter 3. We verified the results of H. k h l q i i i s t for the eccentric wnveforms['29]

and of P.C.Peters[l6] for the radiation reaction.

    Part of the work presented here was original. We obtained the time evolution of the
parameters describing eccentric b i n q systems. the explicit time evolution of the waveforms.
and we showed that the waveforms associated with eccentric binaries possess more features
than the waveforms of circular binary systems (see figure (5.2)). This made the calculations
for eccentric binaries more arduous. Among other things, these features forbid the use
of the stationary phase approximation to calculate the Fourier transforms of the eccentric
waveforms. Instead they were obtained by using a fast Fourier transform algorithm. The
fact that the gravitational waves oscillate a t once, twice and thrice the orbital frequency
plays an essential role: and special attention must be paid to this effect when constructing
adequate signals b r the LIGO interferometers. The presence of these different terms forced
us to start the orbital evolution of the eccentric binaries much earlier than is necessary
for circulas binaries. Once the Fourier transforms were calculated, the optimal SNR for
eccentric binaries was calculated (see figure (5.3)), the ambiguity function was constructed,

and the fitting factor was obtained by rnaximizing the ambiguity function. Our results show

that massive eccentric binaries should be detected by the initial LIGO interferometers as
long as the initial eccentricity is Iower than 0.35. We finci that the fitting factor increases as
the total niass of the system increases (figure (5.5)). This is good news, since as the n i a s of
the binary increases the radiation it enlits is stronger. making the detection easier. It will
be easier, for the initial LIGO, to detect massive binaries whether t hey ase circulu or riot.

6.2 Future Outlook

6.2.1 Post-Newtonian corrections t o the eccentric waveforms

A crucial simplification we made in this work was to assume the validity of the quadrupole
approximation? t hus neglect ing al1 post-Newtonian correct ions. This is an oversimplifica-
tion, becausc the r d waveforrns have a slightly different behavior due to these corrections.
Shough it is doubtful that the results will be qualitatively affected by inciudirig these post-
Newtonian corrections, to include them would be desirable if a fully quantitative analysis

were necessary. The eccentric waveforms have been calculated to order (C) 4 beyond the

leading-order expression by A. Gopakumar and R. Iyer[l3]. Their paper contains the basis
for an improved version of the present work. By including post-Newtonian corrections into
the eccentric signal and the templates, we c m expect the fitting factor to increase slightly
because the templates are characterized by a larger number of parameters. The new pa-
rameten might make it possible to foiiow the phase of the eccentric signals more accurately.
On the other hand, we cannot expect a perfect match (FF= 1) between eccentric signals
and circular templates. There should be a quantitative difference in the fitting factor, but
qualitatively, we expect the same type of c u v e as shown in figure (5.5).

6.2.2 Advanced LIGO

The present work was carried out for the initial LIGO detectors. These are sensitive to
signals with frequencies between 40 Hz and 1000 Hz. It would be interesting to do the cai-
culations for the advanced version of LIGO. This is the detector we are ultiniately interested
in, since it is far more sensitive than the initial LIGO. The frequency range for this detector
will be between 10 Hz and 1000 Hz. Since coalescing compact binaries spend most of their
life in the low frequency regime. the inclusion of the frequency band between 10 Hz and 40
Hz will ruore than double the duration of the signais. To obtain a good frequency resolution,
we would have to use a huge riumber of points and the computing facilities presently at Our
disposition are not sufficient. Nevertheless we can reach some intuitive conclusions froni the
knowledge accurnulated while working in the context of the initial LIGO. kVe know that the
fitting factor increases when the total rnass of the eccentric binary increases due to a shorter
duration of the signal. Le. the less tinie the eccentric signals last. the more accurately the
circular templates cari follow them. Thus, it is alrnost certain that the circulw templates

would not perform as weil for the advanced LIGO as foc the initial LIGO since the duration

of the signal will be longer than the ones displayed in table (4.1). On the other hand,
we can expect the same trends to appear in the fitting factor: For a given binary system,
the fitt ing factor should decreasc as the initial eccentricity increases; and for binaries with
different masses and the same initial eccentricity, the fitting factor should be higher for the
more massive binaries, since its signal will have to be foUowed for a shorter period of time.

    We can also predict an increase in the optimal SNR for eccentric binaries. The increase
observed for the initial LIGO is due to the presence of the three hindamental components
a t once: twice and thrice the orbital frequency. The advanced LIGO will see more of the
eccentric contributions at low frequencies leading to a higher signal-to-noise ratio: The three
components are stronger at Iow frequencies than at high frequencies (see figure (5.2)) and
this should be reflected in the optimal SNR.

6.2.3 Time-frequency detection of gravitational waves

We have seen that non-optimal filtering results in a net loss in signal-to-noise ratio. This
is likely to occur for a variety of sources. It rnight be worth exploring alternative detection
methods that do not rely on a prior knowledge of the sources and their evolution. Such
methods exist, but very little is known about their properties and how they can efficiently be
applied ta the detection of gravitational waves in kilometer-size iriterferometers. One such
method is time-frequency detectiori[33]. Thc detcction process in this case is carried out
in the time-frequency domain. using a filter that does not requirc ariy detailed information
about the sources. This method will not result in the optirnai signal-to-noise ratio, but since
non-optimal filtering results in a, Ioss of signal-to-noise ratio. it might be worth exploring
whether time-frequency methods do better than matched filteririg in the absence of optimal
fil t ers.
